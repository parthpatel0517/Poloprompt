A Practical AI Content Quality Checklist Before You Publish

AI can get a first draft on the page quickly. That is useful, but it does not make the draft ready to publish. A model can write smoothly while getting a fact wrong, flattening an important nuance, or giving advice that is too broad to use. The job after generation is editorial: turn a plausible draft into something specific, accurate, and worth a reader's time.

This checklist works for blog posts, product pages, newsletters, and social copy. A short caption needs a quick voice and claim check; a buying guide, technical article, or high-stakes topic deserves a deeper review. The important part is that a human remains accountable for the published result.

1. Name the reader's actual job

Before editing a line, state the problem the reader hired the page to solve. “People interested in marketing” is not a job. “A small shop owner deciding whether to send a welcome discount on day one” is. If the draft tries to answer three different questions, split it or choose one. Specific intent creates a useful article; broad intent creates a list of familiar advice.

2. Make the opening earn attention

Remove openings that merely announce the topic. “AI is changing content creation” gives a reader no reason to continue. Replace it with a concrete tension, observation, or decision: “A 1,500-word draft is not an article until someone has checked what it assumes.” The goal is not drama. It is to show the reader that this page understands the problem they have.

3. Mark every claim that needs evidence

Read with a highlighter mindset. Numbers, rankings, product capabilities, laws, prices, platform rules, and “most people” claims all need a source or removal. Do not let confident phrasing substitute for proof. If you cannot verify a claim, soften it into clearly labeled opinion, replace it with a confirmed example, or cut it.

It helps to distinguish a fact (“the return policy allows 30 days”), an interpretation (“that policy reduces purchase risk”), and a recommendation (“place the policy near the add-to-cart button”). A strong page tells readers which kind of statement they are reading.

4. Add detail a generic draft cannot supply

Contribute material the model could not know: an observed customer question, a real workflow, a small experiment, a product limitation, or a before-and-after decision. You do not need a grand case study. A short note such as “incomplete form fields were the real cause of routing failures” gives the reader a concrete lesson instead of another abstract rule.

5. Turn conclusions into instructions

“Improve your prompts” and “focus on quality” are conclusions, not instructions. For each major section, ask what a reader should do next. Good advice has a condition, an action, and a way to judge the result. Instead of “use a clear CTA,” write: “Choose one next action, place it after the proof section, and make the button label describe the outcome rather than saying Submit.”

6. Include constraints and exceptions

Generic content often fails because it treats a recommendation as universal. A discount may recover an abandoned cart, but it can train regular buyers to wait. An automation can remove repetitive work, but should not make irreversible customer decisions without review. These caveats make content more credible and help readers avoid applying a tactic in the wrong situation.

7. Make the page easy to scan

Readers should be able to understand a page by reading its headline, subheadings, first sentence of each section, and lists. Use headings that promise a specific answer rather than labels such as “Benefits” or “Conclusion.” Keep paragraphs focused on one idea. Structure is not decoration; it is how a busy reader finds what matters.

8. Replace stock phrasing with precise language

Watch for polished phrases that say little: “unlock,” “seamless,” “revolutionary,” “delve into,” and “leverage.” Usually the fix is not a clever synonym. It is a concrete noun or verb. Replace “unlock efficiency” with “send new form submissions to the right salesperson within five minutes.” Precision improves both voice and comprehension.

9. Read it aloud

Reading aloud exposes overlong sentences, abrupt transitions, and claims you would never make to a customer face to face. It also catches a common AI failure: a draft that sounds certain when its underlying information is uncertain. Helpful is better than hyped; direct is better than vague; honest limitations are better than an overpromise.

10. Finish with a useful next step

A conclusion should help the reader apply what they learned. Summarize the decision, link to the next relevant resource, or offer a small template. Do not repeat the introduction in different words. A five-minute final pass

  • Is the page written for one identifiable reader and task?
  • Are factual and platform-specific claims verified?
  • Did you add a real example, observation, or constraint?
  • Can a reader take a clear next action after each main section?
  • Did you remove repeated ideas, unsupported promises, and empty buzzwords?

AI is most useful as a drafting partner, a way to explore structure, or a way to challenge a blank page. It is not a substitute for subject knowledge, source checking, or editorial judgment. Keep that division of work clear, and the result can be faster to produce without becoming less useful to the people you want to serve.
