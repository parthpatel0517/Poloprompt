AIDA vs. PAS vs. Before-After-Bridge: Which Ad Framework Should You Use?

Most ad copy that performs well is quietly following one of a small number of structural templates, even when it doesn't look like it. Three of these show up more than any others: AIDA, PAS, and Before-After-Bridge. They overlap in places, but they're built for different audience states, and picking the wrong one is a common reason a well-written ad still underperforms.

AIDA: Attention, Interest, Desire, Action

AIDA is the oldest of the three and the most general-purpose. It walks a cold reader through four stages: grab their attention, build interest by explaining what the thing is, create desire by connecting it to a benefit they want, then close with a clear action.

Example — a fictional standing desk brand:

"Still hunched over your laptop at 3pm? (Attention) The Drift Desk adjusts from sitting to standing in four seconds, no app, no motor noise. (Interest) Users report fewer afternoon energy crashes within two weeks of switching. (Desire) Try it for 30 days — free returns if it doesn't change how you work. (Action)"

AIDA works best on audiences who don't yet know they have the problem your product solves, or don't know your product exists. It has to do more educational work up front, which makes it a good fit for cold traffic — people seeing your brand for the first time on social media or display ads.

PAS: Problem, Agitate, Solution

PAS skips the introduction and goes straight for a problem the reader already recognizes, makes it feel more urgent, then presents the product as the resolution. It's shorter and punchier than AIDA because it assumes the reader is already at least a little aware of the pain point.

Example — a fictional invoicing app for freelancers:

"Chasing a client for payment for the third time this month? (Problem) Every unpaid invoice is a loan you didn't agree to give — and the longer it sits, the less likely you'll ever see that money. (Agitate) Invoicely sends automatic payment reminders so you never have to send that awkward follow-up email again. (Solution)"

PAS converts well with problem-aware audiences — people who already feel the pain but haven't found (or committed to) a fix. It's a common choice for retargeting ads, email sequences to warm leads, and any product solving an acute, immediate frustration like late payments, clutter, or a broken workflow.

Before-After-Bridge: Before, After, Bridge

BAB paints the reader's current state, then paints the improved state they want, then shows the product as the bridge connecting the two. It leans harder into transformation and visualization than PAS, which makes it a natural fit for anything selling a change in identity or lifestyle rather than a quick fix.

Example — a fictional beginner running program:

"Right now, running a single mile without stopping feels out of reach. (Before) In eight weeks, picture finishing a 5K at an easy pace, without dreading the run the night before. (After) The Couch-to-5K Method is the structured, day-by-day plan that gets you from one to the other, built for people who've never run consistently in their life. (Bridge)"

BAB is the natural choice for fitness programs, courses, coaching, and anything else where the value is a transformation over time rather than a one-time problem fix. It gives the reader something aspirational to hold onto, which PAS's more clinical problem-solving tone doesn't emphasize as strongly.

Which one should you use?

  • Cold, unaware audiences who don't know your product or may not know they have the problem → AIDA, because it has room to introduce and build the case from scratch.
  • Problem-aware audiences who already feel the pain and are actively looking for a fix → PAS, because it's fast and doesn't waste time on introductions they don't need.
  • Transformation-driven products like fitness, courses, coaching, or anything selling a future identity → Before-After-Bridge, because it sells the destination, not just the fix.

In practice, a lot of high-performing ad copy blends elements of two frameworks — a PAS opening that agitates a problem, followed by a BAB-style vision of the after state, closed with an AIDA-style clear call to action. Treat these as starting structures, not rigid rules.
