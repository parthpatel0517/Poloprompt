How to Get an AI Headshot That Still Looks Like You

The appeal is obvious: a polished, studio-quality headshot for LinkedIn or your website without booking a photographer. The usual result is less appealing. You get a sharply lit, well-dressed person with your hairstyle who isn't quite you. The jaw is a little narrower, the skin a little too smooth, the teeth a little too white. Colleagues notice, and on a profile whose whole purpose is to help people recognize you, that small mismatch quietly costs you trust.

The good news is that most of that drift is caused by the prompt and the source photo, and both are easy to fix.

Why AI headshots drift away from your face

Image models learn from enormous numbers of portraits, and many of the ones labelled "professional" have been retouched. So when a prompt asks for a "flawless", "polished" or "perfect" headshot, the model reads each of those words as permission to pull your features toward that retouched average. Every vague quality word is an invitation to change something.

The source photo matters just as much. A dark, blurry or very close-up selfie leaves gaps in what the model can see, and it fills those gaps with guesses. Wide-angle phone cameras held close to the face also exaggerate the nose and shrink the ears, so the model is starting from a distorted version of you.

Start with a better selfie

  • Face a window. Soft daylight from the front shows your real skin tone and texture. Avoid overhead lights and harsh direct sun.
  • Get the camera further away. Use a timer or ask a friend, and shoot from at least an arm's length at eye level to avoid close-up lens distortion.
  • Show your whole face. No sunglasses, hats or hands near the face, and push hair back from the eyes if it usually covers them.
  • Use the expression you want to keep. If you want a natural smile in the final image, smile naturally in the source photo.
  • Take several and pick the sharpest. Zoom in on the eyes. If they're soft, the result will be too.

The prompt structure that protects your identity

The order of instructions matters. Put identity first, list exactly what is allowed to change, describe the photography in concrete terms, and finish with an explicit list of things the model must not do.

Using the attached selfie, create a professional headshot of the same person.

KEEP EXACTLY: facial structure, eye shape and color, nose, skin tone, natural skin texture, hairline, and [DISTINCTIVE FEATURES, e.g. freckles, glasses, a scar].

CHANGE ONLY: clothing to [OUTFIT]; hair tidied but in the same style; background to [BACKGROUND, e.g. seamless mid-grey studio backdrop].

PHOTOGRAPHY: head and upper shoulders, eyes about one-third from the top, soft key light from front-left with gentle fill, 85mm lens at f/2.8, [EXPRESSION].

DO NOT: slim the face, smooth the skin, whiten teeth, change apparent age, or add makeup or jewelry that isn't in the photo.

Notice what's missing: "flawless", "beautiful", "perfect". Describing the lighting and lens gets you the professional look. Adjectives about attractiveness are what change your face.

Fixing the most common problems

  • You look younger or thinner. Add "keep my apparent age and face width exactly as in the photo" and regenerate.
  • The skin looks like plastic. Ask for "natural skin texture with visible pores and no retouching".
  • Your eye color or smile changed. Name them explicitly in the KEEP list. Anything you don't mention is fair game for the model.
  • Strange hands or shoulders. Tighten the framing to head and upper shoulders so there are no hands in the shot at all.
  • The background is distracting. A plain seamless grey or softly blurred office reads as most professional and gives the model the least to get wrong.

Generate several versions and compare them side by side with your source photo. Pick the one a friend would recognize instantly, not the most flattering one.

Use it honestly

A headshot exists so people can recognize you when they meet you on a video call or in person, so aim for a photo that looks like you on a good day with good lighting, not a different person. If a job application, ID or platform asks for an unedited photo, use a real one. And only use this on photos of yourself, or of people who've clearly agreed to it.
