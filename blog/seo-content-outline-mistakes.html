5 Mistakes People Make When Using AI to Outline SEO Content

AI-generated content outlines have gotten a bad reputation in SEO circles, and mostly for good reason — a lot of them are structurally weak in ways that have nothing to do with the writing quality and everything to do with how they were prompted. Here are the five most common mistakes, and the specific fix for each one.

Mistake 1: Outlining without specifying search intent

A query like "best running shoes" could be informational (a buying guide), commercial (a comparison of specific models), or transactional (a shopper ready to buy right now). Asking an AI to "outline a blog post about best running shoes" without saying which intent you're targeting produces a generic, unfocused structure that tries to do a bit of everything and satisfies none of it well.

Fix: State the search intent explicitly in the prompt — "this is a commercial comparison post for someone comparing specific models before buying, not a beginner's guide to running" — and the outline that comes back will be shaped around the right angle from the first heading.

Mistake 2: Generic H2 labels instead of specific promises

Left unguided, AI outlines gravitate toward vague section headers like "Benefits of X," "Why X Matters," or "Things to Consider." These tell a reader nothing about what they'll actually learn, and they tend to signal thin, undifferentiated content to anyone scanning the page — including, increasingly, to search engines evaluating whether a page adds anything new.

Fix: Ask for headings phrased as specific claims or questions instead of topics — "How Much Cushioning Actually Matters for Marathon Distances" instead of "Benefits of Cushioning." Specific headings force specific content underneath them.

Mistake 3: Ignoring word count distribution across sections

A common failure mode is asking for a "2,000-word article outline" and getting back six sections with no indication of how those 2,000 words should be distributed. The result is often a piece where the writer front-loads word count into the first section and runs out of depth by the end, or spreads it so evenly that a section that deserved 600 words gets 150.

Fix: Ask for an approximate word count next to each section header, based on how much that subtopic typically needs to be covered well. This turns the outline into an actual writing brief instead of just a table of contents.

Mistake 4: Forgetting to ask for a title tag and meta description

Outlines frequently stop at the H1 and jump straight into body sections, leaving the title tag and meta description as an afterthought written separately — or not written at all. Both are ranking- and click-through-relevant, and both are easy to generate at the same time as the outline itself.

Fix: Explicitly request a title tag (under ~60 characters) and meta description (under ~155 characters) as part of the same outline request, not as a separate step you might forget to do later.

Mistake 5: Stuffing secondary keywords unnaturally instead of distributing them

When asked to "include these 10 secondary keywords," AI models sometimes cram several of them into the same section or introduction just to check the box, producing an oddly dense, repetitive-sounding opening while later sections have none. This reads as unnatural to both readers and search engines.

Fix: Ask explicitly for secondary keywords to be distributed one or two per section based on topical relevance, rather than all at once — and specify that each should appear naturally, not forced into a sentence where it doesn't fit.

Put together, these fixes matter more than the model you use

None of these five fixes require a more powerful AI model — they require a more complete prompt. An outline that specifies intent, specific headings, word count distribution, metadata, and natural keyword placement will consistently outperform a "better" model given a vague one-line request.
